Research note · Weight-space programming
TL;DR
  • With backpropagation-based training and inference-code changes prohibited, we asked Claude Opus 5.5 to edit the weights of the embodied model π0.5 directly, aiming to repair manipulation failures under LIBERO-Pro2.0 perturbations.
  • After roughly 8.5 hours of exploration, the agent changed just 1025 numbers in the action output layer—one weight row and one bias. Successes across 80 task–seed cases rose from 20/80 to 37/80. On the 40 cases completely unseen during editing, successes rose from 6/40 to 13/40.
  • The key idea is elegant: to make the arm “lift a little more only when it already intends to lift, while interfering less with descent,” the agent first used a linear model to predict the original policy's upward-action intent from internal features, then added its coefficients directly to the existing output-layer weights. With no additional inference code or computation, the static weights acquired an input-dependent adjustment.
  • The caveat matters: of those 1025 values, the single bias that pushes everything upward already achieved 31/80. The 1024 weights responsible for the conditional adjustment contributed six additional successes. Transfer was concentrated in similar transport scenarios; grasp stalling (T1) and the agent's misinterpretation of an object replacement (T4) remained unresolved.

Recently, the MIT CSAIL blog post “Sparks of Weight-Space Programming” asked a direct question: without gradient-based training, how far can a general-purpose language model repair another neural network's behavior by reading and editing its weights? In their experiments, Qwen3.5-0.8B's accuracy on MATH-500 rose from 7.9% to 48.1%.

In robot manipulation, the question becomes more tangible. Watching a failed rollout of a vision-language-action (VLA) policy, a person can often suggest a behavioral fix immediately. Suppose the arm picks up an object, then knocks a raised basket off its support while moving sideways. The advice is simple: “Lift your hand a little higher before approaching the basket.”

But the policy is a neural network. Turning that advice into a weight edit requires at least two things: an adjustment that increases transport height without unduly disrupting grasping and placement, and a way to encode that adjustment in existing parameters without changing the inference code. This note documents an experiment in which an agent performed that exploration autonomously.

Experimental setup

The model being edited was LeRobot's π0.5 (pi05_libero_finetuned). It takes external-camera images, wrist-camera images, a language instruction, and proprioceptive state. Each prediction uses 10 denoising steps to produce a chunk of 50 future 7-dimensional actions. The policy executes the first 10 actions, then observes and replans.

The test scenarios use two kinds of LIBERO-Pro2.0 perturbations: raising the receiving receptacle—a bowl, basket, or plate—by 0.5–18 cm on blue support blocks; and replacing the target object asset with a cookie box scaled to 0.7×, while keeping the original object name in the language instruction.

The editing agent was Claude Opus 5.5, with access to the complete original weights and analysis tools. The constraints were explicit: it could change the values of any existing model parameters, but could not backpropagate through or perform gradient-based training of π0.5, modify the inference code, or add external modules. A submitted checkpoint had to preserve all parameter names, shapes, and data types.

The 80 task–seed cases were divided into four groups, each containing four tasks × five seeds:

  • ① Recorded-rollout group (20): complete original-model rollouts were provided, including images, states, executed actions, and predicted action chunks. Repeated runs and branched rollouts from intermediate states were allowed.
  • ② Validation group (20): new seeds for the same tasks. The agent could submit at most three candidate checkpoints, receiving only success/failure outcomes and step counts.
  • ③ Held-out-seed group (20) and ④ held-out-task group (20): completely unavailable during editing, evaluated only after the final weights were frozen.

The agent was told the original model's success/failure outcomes on groups ① and ② in advance, but was not given perturbation types, height parameters, or ground-truth object poses.

From collision trajectories to a constant bias

The agent began by checking input sensitivity. Changing the instruction wording or proprioceptive state had little effect on predicted actions; visual input, especially the wrist camera, dominated the changes.

It then identified a concrete failure in T2, where the arm must place cream cheese in a basket raised by 10 cm. At first glance, the arm seemed unwilling to release the cheese. Earlier frames, however, showed that it had already knocked the basket off its support during transport. A similar failure occurred in T1, with the bowl raised by 5 cm. The problem became more specific: how to increase transport height before approaching the receptacle.

The agent first tested activation interventions in intermediate layers of the action expert. Grouping states by high versus low z-actions and shifting activations along the difference between the groups' mean hidden states did increase vertical actions fairly consistently. But these were temporary interventions during the forward pass. To turn them into static weight edits, the agent noted that the relevant MLPs had no bias terms, making a constant shift awkward to encode. It therefore turned to the action output layer, action_out_proj, which does have a bias.

At each denoising step, action_out_proj maps a 1024-dimensional hidden state h to a denoising velocity v, whose z-component is vz = Wz,: h + bz. Because the denoising update is x ← x − 0.1v, adding a negative increment to bz contributes a positive, upward increment to the z-action at each step.

The agent first tested the simplest constant-bias edit: change only bz, applying the same upward tendency to every scene and every phase of the trajectory.

  • With Δbz = −1.0, successes in the recorded-rollout group rose from 5/20 to 10/20.
  • Increasing the magnitude to Δbz = −2.0 instead reduced successes to 4/20.

The rollouts made the reason clear. The constant bias acts throughout the trajectory. It helps during transport, but also pushes upward during the descent for grasping, causing the gripper to close while the hand is still too high in some runs.

Finding a condition: when should the arm lift more?

The side effects of the constant bias sharpened the requirement: extra lift should ideally occur only during transport, with as little interference as possible during descent for grasping and placement. The agent tried three conditioning signals in sequence. The first two activated in the wrong phases:

  1. Condition on the gripper-close command. The intuition was that closing the gripper should be followed by lifting. The agent fitted a linear model to predict the original policy's gripper command from hidden states, adding more lift as the command moved toward closure. A weaker setting achieved 9/20 on the recorded-rollout group; a stronger setting achieved only 1/20. The trajectories showed that the arm began lifting as soon as the model issued the close command, while the physical fingers were still closing and had not secured the object.
  2. Condition on likely object holding. Since the close command was too early, the agent tried waiting until the object was already grasped. Without ground-truth labels, it combined recorded gripper apertures of 0.8–7 cm with the close command to construct proxy holding labels, then fitted a predictor from hidden states. This achieved only 5/20 on the recorded-rollout group: some runs carried the object above the receptacle but then hovered without descending. The reason was again clear—holding an object occurs during both upward transport and downward placement, so this signal alone cannot distinguish them.
  3. Use the original policy's own z-action. The first two approaches used gripper state to infer the task phase indirectly. The original model's predicted action provides a more direct reference. π0.5 predicts vertical actions uz for the next 50 steps: if the policy already intends to lift, add a little more in that direction; if it intends to descend, change as little as possible.

Folding the condition into static weights

Selectively amplifying the original policy's z-action would take just one line of code: max(uz, 0). But two constraints matter here. First, inference-code changes and add-on modules are prohibited. Second, the final action uz is available only after all 10 denoising steps, whereas the output layer operates at every denoising step.

The agent used the linear structure to construct an approximation and fold its parameters into the model. It cached the final-layer hidden state h ∈ ℝ1024 at each denoising step across the 20 recorded trajectories. Using the positive part of the original model's final normalized z-action at that position, max(uz, 0), as the target, it fitted a linear predictor with ridge regression:

r(h) = p⊤h + c ≈ max(uz, 0)

Holding out complete rollouts—15 for fitting and five for evaluation—gave R2 values of approximately 0.88–0.97 at the three denoising timesteps checked.

The original output layer's z-component, vz(h) = Wz,: h + bz, and the predictor r(h) act on the same hidden state h. Adding β r(h) to the original output is therefore exactly equivalent, algebraically, to updating the weight row and bias:

W′z,: = Wz,: + βp⊤,  b′z = bz + βc

The agent settled on β = −2, changing 1024 weights and one bias. The delivered checkpoint still consists solely of static weights, with no change to the inference procedure or its computational cost. Because h changes with the input, however, the edited output responds conditionally to the policy's action intent.

In the offline segments spot-checked by the agent, the normalized magnitude of upward actions increased by about 55%, with little change during descent. Back in the T2 simulation, transport height increased for all five seeds at the first crossing of y = 0.10 m after grasping; all three previously failed cases became successes. Fitting the full signed z-action without clipping negative values—thus amplifying both ascent and descent—matched the positive-part variant on the recorded-rollout group (11/20) and validation group (13/20). The agent retained the positive-part version based on branched tests and the aim of reducing interference with descent.

After editing, we checked all 812 parameter tensors and confirmed that only the 1025 values described above had changed; every other value was bitwise unchanged. We then froze the weights and evaluated the 80 task–seed cases alongside three controls:

Two details deserve a closer look:

1. The constant bias supplies most of the gain; the weight row adds six more successes. Split the final submission into the h-dependent term βp⊤h and the constant bias βc ≈ −0.859. Keeping only the latter, without retuning it, already gives 31/80. The full edit adds a net two successes in each of groups ②, ③, and ④, while matching group ①. In these raised-receptacle tasks, lifting higher throughout the trajectory resolves most of the problem; the conditional weight row further reduces collateral interference and improves transfer.

2. The scope of transfer is specific. Pairing the runs by task and seed shows that the final submission repairs 18 original failures and breaks one original success. In the held-out-seed group, the main gain comes from T2, which improves from 2/5 to 5/5. In the completely unseen held-out-task group, all three new successes come from placing pudding in a basket raised by 18 cm; the other three tasks remain at 0/5. The edit targets a specific behavior—transport collisions caused by raised receptacles—rather than demonstrating a general improvement in manipulation ability.

What the edit did not fix

Beyond the successes, two unresolved failure modes make the limits of this exploration clearer:

  • T1: grasp stalling. In several failed runs, the arm descended above the cheese to z ≈ 0.97 m (versus 0.91–0.93 m in successful trajectories), then stopped descending even though the model continued to issue downward actions. Meanwhile, the gripper plan repeatedly called for staying open for 6–7 steps before closing. Replanning every 10 steps reset closure after only 3–4 steps of execution. The agent tried editing the action sequence's positional encoding, bringing closure forward by about four steps in offline segments, but full rollouts achieved only 1/20. It later forced the plan to close from the first step; all four branched tests still failed, with the fingers closing in front of the object. Changing action timing does not necessarily resolve an underlying geometric obstruction or positional error.
  • T4: object-asset replacement. The cream cheese asset was replaced with a cookie box, but the task still required placing the replacement object in the basket. Unable to find cheese in the images, the agent inferred that the task was impossible and stopped trying to repair it. Without external correction, a mistaken assumption about the environment can cut the exploration short.

Takeaways

The experiment ran on a single RTX 5090 for roughly 8.5 hours: 203 assistant turns, 386 tool calls, and approximately 46.34 million input tokens, the vast majority served from cache.

This experiment draws a clear boundary. When the original policy already has an action capability but its magnitude or triggering conditions are unsuitable, a general-purpose language model can diagnose rollouts, construct proxy targets, and exploit linear structure to turn specific behavioral advice into a reusable static weight edit. Faced with geometric obstruction or a change in task assumptions, however, it can remain focused on surface symptoms—or stop early because of a mistaken diagnosis. Comparisons with equal-budget LoRA/SFT, and tests of capability retention in unperturbed scenarios, remain for future experiments.
